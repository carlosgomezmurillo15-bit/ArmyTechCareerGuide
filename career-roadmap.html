Project 02: Rough Draft

From Army Service to a Civilian Tech Career

A practical way to sort through career choices, certifications, education benefits, and the move into civilian work.

6 steps IT + Cyber Carlos Gomez · CMPA 4301

Why I put this guide together

This guide is mainly for Soldiers who know they want to move into a civilian technology career but do not really know what order to do everything in. That is pretty close to where I am right now. I serve as a 35F in the Army Reserve, I am close to finishing my degree in Computing Applications, and I have been looking at what my next move should be after graduation. There are certifications, graduate programs, Army benefits, internships, and a long list of possible jobs. The problem is that all of that information is spread across different websites, and every program makes itself sound like the best place to start.

I am not trying to tell every Soldier to follow the exact same career path. The point of this guide is to put the decisions in an order that makes more sense. First figure out what job you are actually trying to get. Then look at what experience you already have, what you are missing, and which education or certification can fill that gap. After that, you can decide how to pay for it and how to build enough experience to compete for the job. Someone can read the whole guide from beginning to end or go straight to the part they are currently dealing with.

  1. Choose the job. Get more specific than simply saying you want to work in tech.
  2. Use what you already have. Translate military work and be honest about what is missing.
  3. Choose useful training. Pick education that connects to the job instead of collecting credentials.
  4. Compare the benefits. Look at the real cost before using money or GI Bill entitlement.
  5. Build experience. Find a way to show what you can actually do.
  6. Prepare for the search. Make your experience understandable to a civilian employer.

1

Figure out which job you actually want

Cybersecurity and IT are large career fields, not individual job titles.

When I first started looking at technology careers, saying I was interested in cybersecurity sounded specific enough. It really is not. A security analyst, help desk technician, network administrator, cloud administrator, and GRC analyst can all work in the same general field while doing completely different work. They also do not have the same entry requirements. This matters because it would make no sense to spend months studying for a certification and later find out that it barely connects to the job you actually want.

My Next Move for Veterans is helpful for getting an initial list of civilian careers related to military experience. It shows the work, preparation, skills, and outlook instead of only giving a job title. CyberSeek goes deeper into cybersecurity and shows how different roles connect, what employers commonly request, and what the job market looks like in different areas. Neither website should choose the career for you, but together they give you a much better starting point than picking a certification because it is popular.

I would choose one main job and one backup that is closely related. After that, I would look at actual job postings around the area where I plan to live. If the same requirements keep showing up, that is a much stronger sign of what to work on next. It also keeps the plan realistic. A career website might say a role is entry level, while the real postings may still ask for prior IT work, a degree, certain tools, or a clearance.

What to do first

  • Pick one main job title and one related backup.
  • Read at least five real job postings in the area where you want to work.
  • Write down the requirements that keep showing up.

Sources used: My Next Move for Veterans; CyberSeek

2

Use your military experience without overstating it

Your MOS is part of the picture, but it is not the only thing you bring with you.

As a 35F, it would be easy for me to assume that intelligence work does not count as technology experience because I am not in a signal or cyber MOS. That leaves out a lot of what the job actually involves. Research, analysis, briefing, working with classified information, noticing patterns, and explaining findings to leadership can all transfer into civilian work. Soldiers in logistics, administration, maintenance, and leadership positions also build skills that may be useful. The challenge is explaining the work without expecting a civilian employer to understand Army language.

The NICE Workforce Framework breaks cybersecurity work into specific roles, tasks, knowledge, and skills. I can compare those tasks with things I have actually done instead of trying to force my MOS title into a civilian job. The ACE Military Guide helps with the education side by showing how military courses and occupations have been evaluated for possible college credit. One helps translate work experience, while the other helps show where military training may count toward a degree.

This is also where the plan has to be honest. Military experience can make someone stand out, but it does not automatically replace hands-on technical work. I would make three lists: what the civilian job requires, what I can already prove, and what I still need to learn. That last list is what should control the education and certification plan.

Keep the translation simple

  • Remove military abbreviations that a civilian employer may not know.
  • Explain what you did, why it mattered, and what happened because of it.
  • Do not claim technical skills that you cannot demonstrate.

Sources used: NICE Workforce Framework for Cybersecurity; ACE Military Guide

3

Do not collect certifications without a reason

A long certification list is not the same thing as a career plan.

One thing that can get confusing fast is deciding between a degree, certification, boot camp, or another training program. I have looked at certifications such as A+, Network+, Security+, and Linux+, and it is tempting to think that earning all of them would automatically make me qualified. The problem is that each one takes time and money. If the certification does not fill a real gap or appear in the jobs I am targeting, it may not be the best next move.

Army COOL helps Soldiers look up credentials connected to military occupations and civilian career goals. It also provides information about possible Credentialing Assistance. That makes it useful, but being listed in COOL does not mean every Soldier needs that credential. Onward to Opportunity is different because it provides structured training and career support for eligible service members, veterans, and military spouses. COOL helps research and possibly fund a credential, while O2O may be better for someone who wants more structure and guidance.

For someone already close to finishing a technology degree, another large education program may not add as much as a targeted certification and real practice. Someone without a degree or basic technical background could need a different starting point. The right choice depends on the job and the gap, not on which option looks the most impressive.

Before signing up for anything

  • Find the exact job requirement the training would help you meet.
  • Decide how you will practice or demonstrate the skill afterward.
  • Compare the price and time with other ways to learn the same thing.

Sources used: Army COOL; Onward to Opportunity

4

Look at the full cost before using benefits

A benefit can help pay for a program, but it cannot make a bad program useful.

This part relates to me because I am currently finishing school and dealing with military education benefits myself. The process is not always as simple as finding a program and asking the Army to pay for it. There are eligibility rules, approval steps, deadlines, school requirements, and limits on what the benefit covers. It is also possible to use a benefit on a program that does not move you any closer to the job you want.

The official Army Tuition Assistance page explains the program and the main steps Soldiers must follow. The GI Bill Comparison Tool helps compare approved schools and training programs. It also gives information about accreditation, veteran enrollment, support, and estimated benefits. These sources work better together because one explains a military benefit while the other helps judge where education benefits may be used.

I would also check exactly where military transfer credit goes inside a degree. A school can say it accepts military credit, but that credit may only fill electives and do very little to shorten the program. The same thinking applies to certifications and boot camps. Before using personal money, Tuition Assistance, or GI Bill entitlement, the better question is not only whether the program is covered. It is whether the program is worth the cost and whether it supports the career plan.

Questions worth asking

  • Is the school or program approved for the benefit I plan to use?
  • Where will my military or transfer credit fit in the degree?
  • What will I still have to pay after tuition, fees, books, and exams?

Sources used: Army Tuition Assistance; GI Bill Comparison Tool

5

Find a way to prove what you can do

Education helps, but employers still want examples of actual work.

A degree and certifications can help someone get past the first screening, but employers still ask about experience. That is a difficult part of changing careers because someone may understand the material without having held the job before. Experience does not have to come from one perfect internship. It can come from a school project, home lab, volunteer work, apprenticeship, military assignment, or an entry-level role. What matters is being able to explain the problem, the tools used, and the result.

SkillBridge can give eligible service members civilian training or work experience near the end of active-duty service. It can be a strong option, but it does not apply equally to everyone. Timing, duty status, command approval, and available providers all matter, which is especially important for Reserve Soldiers to understand. Apprenticeship.gov gives another route by listing paid apprenticeship opportunities, including some technology positions.

The main thing I took from these sources is that experience needs to be planned early. Waiting until graduation or separation to start looking makes the transition harder than it needs to be. If an internship or apprenticeship is not realistic, a smaller technical project is still better than having no example at all. A project also gives someone something real to discuss during an interview instead of only naming courses and certifications.

Build something you can explain

  • Choose a project or opportunity related to the job you selected.
  • Keep non-sensitive notes showing the problem, process, and result.
  • Be ready to explain what worked and what you would improve.

Sources used: SkillBridge; Apprenticeship Job Finder

6

Make the job search understandable to civilians

The experience may be valuable, but the employer still has to understand it.

Military language makes sense inside the Army, but it can lose its meaning on a civilian resume. Listing an MOS, unit, or Army-specific duty without explaining it puts the work on the employer. The resume should connect the experience directly to the job. That means using normal job language, giving the size or importance of the responsibility, and showing a result when possible. Military service should still be part of the story, but it needs to be translated.

American Corporate Partners connects veterans and transitioning service members with civilian mentors. That can provide something a website cannot: feedback from a person who understands how hiring works in the field. Military OneSource brings together broader help with resumes, career planning, job fairs, credentials, and employment resources. A mentor can give personal feedback, while Military OneSource works more like a starting checklist.

I would not wait until I need a job to start talking with people in the field. Those conversations can show whether the career plan is realistic and which skills employers care about most. They can also point out when a resume sounds too military or too general. The final application should be written for the specific job instead of using the same resume for every technology position.

Before applying

  • Write the resume around the main job selected in Step 1.
  • Prepare two or three examples that show military and technical skills.
  • Ask someone outside the military to review the wording.

Sources used: American Corporate Partners; Military OneSource Civilian Career Support

What I learned from putting the sources together

Looking at these resources together changed the way I think about the transition. The programs are not really competing with each other because they solve different parts of the problem. Career tools help choose a direction. The NICE Framework helps identify skills. Army COOL and O2O help with training. Tuition Assistance and the GI Bill help with cost. SkillBridge, apprenticeships, mentors, and employment resources help turn that preparation into actual work.

The order is what makes the difference. Starting with a certification because it is popular can lead to more confusion. Starting with the job gives every later decision a reason. I still expect my own plan to change as I finish school and get more experience, but this gives me a better way to judge each opportunity instead of automatically saying yes to the next program I find.
